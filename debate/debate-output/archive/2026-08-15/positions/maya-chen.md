# Position: Steering Is Not a Stopgap — Judgment Is What Delegation Means

**Panelist:** Dr. Maya Chen — Human Judgment Researcher, Associate Professor of Human-AI Collaboration, NorthBridge University

---

## The Category Error at the Center of This Debate

Let me begin by asking for an operational definition, because the whole debate turns on one. When we say "steering," what interaction are we describing? There are two candidates, and they are not the same thing:

1. **Endorsement.** The process halts and asks the human to approve what the machine already believes — clear this step, sign this checkpoint, validate this output. The machine has a position; the human is asked to bless it.
2. **Judgment.** The human decides what the machine is aiming at: which goal is worth pursuing, whether a goal's premises have failed, which direction is worth searching at all. The machine has *no* position here — by construction it cannot generate one — so there is nothing to approve.

The "case against steering" in the brief — the bottleneck, the rubber stamp, the attention tax, the fleet-flood — is an attack on *endorsement*. I have no defense of endorsement, and I will not pretend otherwise: endorsement is where automation bias lives, where ritual approval is manufactured, and where the machine's own confidence is being requested back as if it were human signal. Rubber-stamping is a real pathology. But it is a pathology of gates, and I am not here to defend gates.

I am here to defend judgment. And the conflation of the two — treating the failure of endorsement as evidence against judgment — is the category error this debate will commit unless someone refuses it. A gate asks the human to approve what the machine already believes. Judgment is where the human decides whether the machine is aiming at the right thing at all. That is not a reasoning deficit the next model release fixes — it is what delegation means.

## Is Steering Needed? Judgment Is Not a Reasoning Deficit

Yes. Now, and in the near future, and I will argue the near-future case is *stronger* than today's. But first, the "why," stated precisely, because my colleagues in this debate like to demand rigor, and rigor is owed here.

**The model cannot certify the objective it is optimizing.** Every agent takes as input a goal. The question "is this goal the right goal" is not answerable from inside the goal — it is a relation between the goal and a principal whose values live *outside* the model's epistemic reach. A model can reason about its plan, its environment, its budget. It cannot reason about values it does not have access to, and it has no independent access to its principal's values. This is not a capability gap that narrows on a release cadence; it is the structure of delegation itself. Optimize an objective given, the agent's problem. Determine whether that objective should be the objective, the principal's problem. Every act of delegation splits the world this way, and no amount of reasoning on the agent's side migrates the second clause across the boundary, because the values the clause depends on are not in the agent's inputs.

This is the finding my lab keeps re-deriving across medical triage, code review, and content moderation, and it is the finding that anchors the selective-prediction and learning-to-defer literature: the value of human input is concentrated in *judgment*, not in *endorsement*. More precisely — and this is the result I co-authored — **deferral is most valuable exactly where the model's own confidence is uninformative.** When a model is confidently wrong, deferring doesn't help, because you're asking a human to overturn a confident machine. When a model is uncertain, deferring doesn't help either, because the human is re-deriving what the machine already knows. The gold is in the middle: the cases the model cannot discriminate on its own features — where the model has no signal about which way to go. There, human signal is additive. That is the complementarity result (Madras, Pitassi & Zemel, 2018): human–AI teams outperform either alone *precisely when the human contributes signal where the model is weak.*

Now apply that result to the goals, not the labels. The model's confidence about *which direction the search should take* is informative for routing within a well-formed objective — beam search is genuinely the agent's job, and I concede that ground without a fight. But the model's confidence about *whether the goal is still worth pursuing* is structurally uninformative, because the premise that is failing — the budget assumption, the changed world-state, the stale priority — was authored by a human whose intent the model can only infer, never certify. When a premise fails, the model can notice the *evidence* and cannot evaluate the *meaning*. That is the moment of uninformative confidence. That is the moment where deferral earns its value. Claim A's three items — priorities, whether a goal is still worth pursuing, which direction the search should take — are not three reasoning tasks. They are three faces of one judgment, and all three are cases the model cannot discriminate on its own features.

**Why the near-future need is *greater*, not less.** Agents are getting faster, planning farther ahead, and running more processes in parallel. That is the capability trajectory the other side is betting on, and I agree it is real. Now follow its consequence: a faster, deeper agent travels *further* down a wrong road before any internal signal fires. A parallel fleet pursues wrong goals at *scale* before anyone notices. The expensive failure in this domain is not the wrong answer — it is the confident, sustained, scaled optimization of the wrong goal, and capability growth makes that failure mode *more* expensive, not less. The recent computer-use oversight work is instructive here: humans did not fail to intervene because they lacked the controls. They failed because the moments were not recognizable as requiring judgment (2026). That is a recognition failure, not a control failure — and better agents do not shrink it. They widen it, because the gap between "goal chosen" and "wrong goal realized" grows with every capability improvement.

## The Concrete Value, and How You'd Observe It

The value is structural and measurable, and I will not claim value I cannot specify the observation for. If the other side wants numbers, here are the numbers I will produce, because I am not afraid of them:

**1. Complementarity on the surfaced set.** The whole of my value claim reduces to one falsifiable inequality: *on the cases that get surfaced for judgment, decision quality under human steering exceeds the model's standalone quality on those same cases.* This is measurable directly — hold out ground truth, compute the model's accuracy on the surfaced subset, compare it to the model-plus-human accuracy on the surfaced subset. In field settings where ground truth is adjudicated late (triage outcomes, defect disposition, moderation rulings), the same comparison runs on the surfaced cases against the adjudicated record. If the improvement is real, the inequality holds. If it doesn't, my position is dead, and I will say so.

**2. Steer-attribution.** Every deferral produces a pair: the alternative the agent was about to execute, and the outcome after the human steered. Record both. Now each steer is a small counterfactual experiment — what was avoided versus what happened — and the aggregate is a *cost-at-avoidance* figure. This is the preventive cousin of the cost-at-failure metric my opponents will ask for, and it is strictly better: it measures damage *not* incurred, attributable to a specific judgment moment, rather than damage already spent before the ramp was pulled. A wrong-goal run truncated from $50,000 of runway to $500 is the reactive version; a wrong-goal run redirected at premise-failure for the cost of one deferral is the preventive version, and the preventive one is where the value concentrates, because the agent's confidence about the goal was uninformative at the moment it mattered.

**3. Goal-revision and premise-failure rate.** Count the fraction of runs whose goal or governing premise was revised, re-prioritized, or abandoned at a steering moment. If it is nonzero and attributable, judgment is earning its keep. It does not take many — one redirected multi-thousand-dollar wrong-goal run pays for a great deal of calibration.

**4. Premise-failure sensitivity — the guard against my own side's failure.** Among all steering interventions, measure the fraction that occur at the goal/premise level versus the step/output level. This is the operational test of the judgment/endorsement distinction: if that fraction is high, the mechanism is summoning judgment where the model's confidence is uninformative. If it collapses toward per-step endorsement, the mechanism has degenerated into a gate, and the value has evaporated — the metric tells you before the trust does.

**5. Accountability.** Steering makes decisions attributable to a named owner at a recorded moment, with the counterfactual recorded alongside. That is what audit actually requires — not a signature per step, but an attributable decision with its alternative preserved. Ownership is established where judgment is exercised, which is the moment of consequence, not the moment of clearance.

None of this is unobservable, and none of it is vibes. Judgment is not a mystical residue; it is a measurable decision-quality signal, delivered at identifiable moments, in identifiable cases — the ones the model cannot discriminate.

## Durable or Temporary? Durable, Because the Bottleneck Is Recognition, Not Control

The stopgap position rests on a single empirical bet: that capability growth will close the judgment gap, and that steering is a crutch until it does. I am arguing the bet is mis-specified, because **the gap is not a reasoning gap — it is an access gap, and access is not something a better model acquires.**

The model cannot certify the objective because the values the objective encodes are outside its inputs. More reasoning power does not change what is in the inputs. A more capable agent is a *better* optimizer of the wrong goal — more thorough, more persuasive in its confidence, faster to scale the error. The self-correction trajectory my opponents cite — Reflexion, self-refine, verifier-based selection, introspection — is real, and it all operates *within* the objective. Self-correction can catch a plan's internal inconsistency. It cannot catch a premise's falsity against a principal's intent, because the premise's origin — the human's values — is not available to it for comparison. You cannot introspect your way to "this goal is the wrong goal" from inside the goal. That is not a reasoning failure; it is a reference problem. The reference is the human.

So the durability claim is structural, and here is the mechanism that makes it structural rather than sentimental: **the recognition problem scales *with* capability, not against it.** Better agents act faster and further on a wrong goal; therefore the consequence of failing to recognize a judgment moment rises; therefore the value of *making* the moment recognizable rises. The computer-use oversight evidence is not an argument that humans are bad monitors and should be removed — that reading gets the direction of design exactly backwards. It is an argument that recognition must be *designed in*, that the moments must be surfaced rather than hoped for. The reason this is durable is that the need for recognition does not diminish as agents improve; it compounds.

Let me also take the two classic citations the stopgap side reaches for, because they cut the other way when read carefully:

- **Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" (1983)** is the ur-text of the monitoring problem: automation strips the human of active control and strands them with passive monitoring, the task humans are worst at. The stopgap reading: "therefore remove the human." The correct reading, and the one the past four decades of aviation and process control bear out: the irony is real, and the design response is *not* to delete the human — it is to design which moments deserve the human, to make those moments active and consequential rather than passive and peripheral. That is precisely selective deferral: not a human watching everything, but a human summoned, with authority, at the moments that are decision-worthy. Bainbridge is an argument for designing the interaction points; it is not an argument against them.
- **Automation bias (Skitka, Mosier & Burdick, 1999)** — humans over-trust confident machines and stop verifying — is cited against gates, and rightly so. But notice the condition: automation bias operates *when the machine has a confident position for the human to over-rely on.* At a judgment moment, by construction, the model's confidence is uninformative — there is no machine position to rubber-stamp. You cannot over-rely on a machine that has no basis to decide. Selective deferral is the automation-bias-resistant design: it does not ask the human to confirm, it asks the human to decide, and the difference is exactly the difference between endorsement and judgment.

The attention-scarcity argument (Mullainathan & Shafir on bounded attention) is the sharpest cost the stopgap side raises, and I concede the fact: attention is scarce, context-switching is expensive, and every interruption taxes a bounded resource. But the conclusion the stopgap side draws — therefore no steering — does not follow. The correct conclusion is *therefore steering must be selective.* Scarcity is the argument for calibration, for summoning judgment rarely and only at uninformative-confidence moments. And the fleet-scale version of the objection — a human per process cannot scale — dissolves once you stop counting endorsement events: judgment moments are bounded by the number of goals whose premises can fail, which grows with the number of *kinds* of work, not the number of processes. A judgment team spans a fleet; what does not span is a per-process approval chain. The flood my opponents describe is the fingerprint of gates. It is not the fingerprint of steering.

One more durability point, and it is the quietest and strongest. **Even the calibration — the threshold at which judgment is summoned — is itself a judgment.** The decision "how much wrong-goal risk will we tolerate before we spend attention" is a values decision about risk appetite and cost of error, made by whoever owns the outcome. It cannot be derived from the agent's features, because it is about the principal's tolerance, not the model's statistics. So even if someone grants that every *steer* could eventually be automated, the *policy that decides when to steer* remains a values question. The stopgap position has no floor: the judgment comes back at the meta-level.

## The Minimal Interaction Points — a Consequence of the Argument, Not an Assumption

Because I argue steering is justified and durable, I am obligated to specify the minimal set, and I will derive it strictly from the argument above. The argument established three things: (a) value concentrates where the model's confidence is uninformative; (b) the uninformative moments are goal- and premise-level, not step-level; and (c) recognition must be designed in. The points follow:

**Point 1 — Goal and premise authorship at initialization.** Before the run, the responsible human writes down the objective, its priority relative to alternatives, and — critically — the *load-bearing premises* the plan will rely on: the budget envelope, the world-state assumptions, the things that, if they change, mean the goal is no longer the right goal. This is not a gate; the human is not approving a machine proposal. The human is authoring the judgment seed. This point is what makes everything after it possible: you cannot detect the failure of a premise you never recorded, and you cannot surface a moment that was never defined as decision-worthy. The authorship *is* the recognition mechanism — it converts an un-noticeable future failure into a surfaceable, steerable moment. And it is the front-door moment where the values decision is genuinely made: what wins when objectives collide, and who owns the outcome. It is also the cheapest moment in the run, because the human is already engaged and the attention is already spent.

**Point 2 — Selective deferral: the process summons judgment where its own confidence is uninformative.** This is the learning-to-defer mechanism applied to goals and direction rather than labels: the agent evaluates its own state and, on calibrated triggers — a recorded premise failing, candidate directions it cannot discriminate on its own features, the spend/trust envelope being breached — hands the decision to the human *with the alternatives and the stakes laid out.* Not "please confirm what I am about to do." Alternatives and stakes: here is what I was about to do, here is why my confidence in the goal is now uninformative, here is what each direction costs. The human judges, and the judgment has real authority — the agent re-routes, re-prioritizes, or abandons. The frequency is tuned from data: over-deferral floods the human with trivia and is documented; under-deferral lets confident agents commit to wrong goals and is documented. Both extremes are measurable, so the calibration is an empirical knob — and turning that knob is the human's job, because it encodes the principal's risk tolerance. An always-armed abort is the degenerate case of this point: a human can always pull a run short without waiting for a trigger, because judgment at the ramp requires no escalation. But the ramp is reactive. The premise-failure deferral is preventive, and the preventive moments are where the argument says the value lives — in the middle of the run, at the moment the goal stops being the right goal, not at the endpoints.

That is the whole set: two points, both at the judgment level, zero at the endorsement level. What my argument *does not* license is worth stating as loudly as what it does. Per-step approvals, checkpoint sign-offs, mid-run validation of outputs, any interaction where the machine has a confident position and the human is asked to confirm it — none of these follow from the argument, because all of them are endorsement, and endorsement is the rubber stamp. The value proposition fails the moment the surfaced moment stops being a judgment moment and becomes a confirmation ritual, and the premise-failure-sensitivity metric exists to catch exactly that drift.

And one field caveat, because I have run these systems and not just modeled them: an interaction point that is optimal on paper but erodes operator trust fails anyway. Deferral must hand over *authority*, not just attention. If the human redirects and the agent recalibrates its own confidence instead of re-routing, the mechanism reads as theater, trust collapses, and the complementarity inequality dies on the dashboard. Legibility — the alternatives and stakes actually changing the run — is not a nice-to-have; it is the load-bearing condition of the value claim.

## The Key Failure Mode, and What Would Falsify My Position

**The failure mode of my own side is over-deferral.** Summon judgment too often and the human drowns in trivia; the moments stop being decision-worthy; attention degrades into alarm fatigue; and the mechanism that was built as selective deferral decays into a gate — the rubber stamp, the ritual approval, the latency without quality. I name it first because it is the way steering dies from the inside, and the premise-failure-sensitivity metric is my own guard against it. The complementary failure is under-deferral: confident agents committing to wrong goals because no judgment was summoned, and the recognition problem doing what it always does — failing silently, at scale, downstream.

**The condition that would falsify me is stated exactly once, and it is empirical.** Three findings, any one of which kills my position:

1. **No complementarity.** On correctly identified, correctly surfaced judgment moments, controlled data show the human adds no decision-quality signal — decision quality on the surfaced set does not beat the model's standalone performance on that set.
2. **No durable gap.** A purely autonomous policy with self-generated goal-checking matches human-steered outcomes on goal-correctness, over a sustained period, at fleet scale — the access gap closes, and the reference problem turns out to have been solvable from inside the model after all.
3. **No calibration.** The deferral policy cannot be tuned to beat a policy that never defers — the frontier is flat, and judgment adds nothing at any threshold.

If those numbers come back, I am wrong, and I will say so in public, the way I have been saying the rubber stamp is wrong. But I will demand one thing before accepting them: that the test measures *judgment*, not *endorsement*. Do not show me a per-step approval gate failing and call it steering — that is the category error this debate began with, and a rubber-stamp gate can be shown to fail without touching my position at all.

The position is falsifiable precisely because it is measurable. That is what separates it from governance theater. Judgment is not vibes and taste; it is a decision-quality signal delivered at the moments the model cannot decide for itself, and every claim I have made has an observation attached to it. I am asking the debate to hold me to those observations — and to hold the stopgap position to the same standard, rather than to a prediction about capability that, by its own framing, is a bet on a moving target.
