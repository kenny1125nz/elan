# Position: Steering Is Needed Because an Outcome No One Owns Is Not an Outcome — It Is an Incident

**Panelist:** Elena Petrova — Governance & Accountability Lead, Director of AI Oversight, Meridian Bank Group

---

## The Question This Debate Keeps Refusing to Ask

Both claims in the brief are capability arguments. Claim A says the human supplies judgment the agent lacks today. Claim B says the agent acquires that judgment next quarter, so steering is a crutch. My colleagues have split along the same line: Kai argues the gap is closing and steering is a bridge; Maya argues the gap is structural and steering is judgment. Both are arguing about *whether the machine needs the human to do the work*.

I am here to tell you that this is the wrong question, and I can tell you why from the seat I sit in. I run the AI oversight function for a bank-grade institution. My job is to prove to regulators and audit committees that decisions made by agents can be attributed, explained, and reversed — before the incident, not after. And I have never once been asked, in an examination, "is your agent smart enough to decide this itself?" I have been asked, in every single examination, a different question:

**"Who decided this? On what basis? When? And how do you reverse it?"**

That question is not about capability. It does not disappear when the agent gets better at reasoning — it gets *louder*, because the actions get bigger and faster. The question the debate should be deciding is not whether human steering beats agent autonomy at producing good outcomes. It is whether a process that no human steers can have an owner. And the answer is no. Not "no, for now." No, as in there is no mechanism by which a fully autonomous process acquires an owner. Ownership has to be manufactured, it has to be manufactured by a human decision at a specific moment, and the moment cannot be reconstructed after the fact.

Steering is needed because **accountability is the non-negotiable**, and accountability requires an attributable human decision. That requirement does not weaken as capability grows. It tightens. A fleet of agents increases the number of decisions that must be owned — it does not decrease it. This is my position, and I will ground it in the regulatory record rather than in vibes about judgment or confidence about quarterly releases.

## Is Steering Needed? Yes — Because Ownership Cannot Be Manufactured Retroactively

Let me be precise about the move I am making, because it separates me from both of my co-panelists, and I want it on the record.

Kai's position concedes accountability in one sentence and then spends it on a signature at the door: the owner is "named at the door," and everything else is "audit of telemetry." This is the exact argument I have spent my career stress-testing as a former regulator, and it does not survive. Naming a principal who pressed "go" on Tuesday is *not* accountability for a consequential decision the agent makes on Thursday that materially changes the risk profile — that decides a counterparty exposure, that authorizes a spend past the envelope, that commits the firm to a position. Attributing Thursday's decision to Tuesday's launch is a governance fiction. The first question in any examination or any litigation is "who decided this, at the moment it was decided, with what information and what authority?" Telemetry tells you what the *agent* did. It does not tell you who *decided*. There is a categorical difference between a record made when a decision is made and a reconstruction of a machine's behavior after the fact. I have watched investigators struggle through both. Only one of them answers the question.

The reason there is a categorical difference is not technical. It is legal. **The law attributes responsibility to persons, not to machines.** A machine cannot be fined. It cannot be examined. It cannot be removed from office or held to a senior-manager certification. It cannot testify. Liability lands on humans — the firm, the accountable executive, the named owner. That is not an accident of current technology; it is the structure of every accountability regime that governs consequential action. And that structure means there must *be* a person who decided, and the decision must be attributable at the moment of consequence — not inferable from a launch authorization and a log.

This is not an abstraction. It is the entire regulatory stack I operate under, and every piece of it is moving toward *more* attribution requirements, not fewer:

- **SR 11-7** (the Fed and OCC's model risk management guidance) requires that models in use — and the decisions taken from them — have documented purpose, approval, and effective challenge. "Nobody decided, the agent ran" is not a surviving answer in a model-risk examination.
- **GDPR Article 22** gives individuals the right not to be subject to *solely automated* decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects. The law draws a line at sole automation and says: at that line, a human decision must exist. That line is not a capability prediction. It is a legal fact.
- **The EU AI Act** mandates human oversight for high-risk systems as a design requirement — not a nice-to-have, a design requirement.
- **The UK Senior Managers & Certification Regime** makes named senior managers personally accountable for their areas of responsibility. When an agent acts in a governed area, there must be a named manager who can answer for it. The regime exists precisely because "the machine did it" is not a defense.
- **BCBS 239** requires that risk data be aggregated and reported so that what happened can be reconstructed. The reconstruction has to be possible, which means the record has to exist — and the record has to include the decision, not just the execution.

Every one of these is capability-independent. You cannot optimize your way out of GDPR Article 22. You cannot train your way out of SR 11-7. These are not bets on a moving target; they are requirements written by institutions that decided, a long time ago, that when something goes wrong at scale, someone must be answerable. The agent improving its planning does not change that someone must be answerable. It changes how much there is to answer for.

Now the near-future question, because the brief asks it directly. **Steering is needed now, and the near-future case is stronger, not weaker.** Here is the mechanism, and it is the same mechanism that makes my durability argument: agents are becoming more consequential, faster, and more numerous. A bigger, faster agent travels further down the wrong road before anyone notices — that is not an argument for less ownership, it is an argument for *more*, because the decision surface grows. Right now, agents are doing bounded tasks under a human who launched them, and the governance deficit is contained. The near future is fleets executing consequential actions across portfolios, markets, and customer flows. Every one of those actions is a decision. And in a fully autonomous process, every one of those decisions has no author. The governance deficit grows faster than the capability curve, because the deficit scales with the *number and size of consequential decisions*, and capability growth increases both.

And let me take the scale objection head-on, because it is the sharpest tool in the stopgap kit: a human per process cannot scale to fleets. Agreed. I am not defending a human per process, and I never was. Ownership does not require a human per process. It requires an **owner per domain of consequential decisions** — a named accountable person for a line of business, a portfolio, a risk domain, a customer flow — with authority limits pre-committed and decision points surfaced only when a pre-committed trigger fires. The number of owners scales with the number of *kinds* of consequential decisions, not with the number of processes. A named owner and a small, trained decision team span a fleet; a per-process approval chain does not. The flood Kai describes is the fingerprint of the per-process gate, and I will not defend the per-process gate. The flood is not an argument against ownership; it is an argument for designing the decision surface — which is what the minimal interaction points in this position actually are.

## The Concrete Value — and How You Would Observe It

My colleagues are measuring the wrong things, and I want to be charitable before I say it, because they are measuring *something*. Kai wants cost-at-failure — dollars of damage truncated by a human pull on the ramp. Maya wants decision-quality complementarity on the surfaced set — the human beating the model on the cases the model can't discriminate. Both of these measure the *quality of the run*. They ask "did steering make the outcome better?" They are legitimate questions, and I do not dispute the metrics.

But neither metric survives the incident. When a wrong-goal run goes to $400,000 of loss and the regulator sits across the table, the questions are not "how much did you truncate the damage?" and "was the human's judgment better than the model's on the surfaced cases?" The questions are: **who decided this, on what basis, when did anyone with authority know, and what did they decide about it?** Cost-at-failure tells you the magnitude of the event. It tells you nothing about whether the event has an owner. Complementarity tells you whether the human added signal. It tells you nothing about whether the decision has an author.

The value steering provides is that it **converts agent behavior into something the organization can own**: a decision with an author, a timestamp, a basis, recorded alternatives, and a defined path to reversal. That is the value. And here is how you observe it — not in the smooth run, but in the record that survives the incident:

1. **Forensic reconstructability.** Of N consequential incidents, how many can be fully reconstructed — who decided, on what basis, at what moment, with what authority, and what alternatives were recorded and rejected? This is the regulator's first question and the investigator's first need. Target: complete reconstruction of every consequential decision, not a probabilistic approximation from telemetry. Measurable directly, across every incident in the register.

2. **Attribution completeness.** Every consequential decision — as defined by the pre-committed trigger set, not by hindsight — has a named author, a timestamp, a recorded basis, the alternatives considered, and the rollback action armed. Measurable as coverage of the consequential-decision set. This is the "what record does this leave" test, and it is the only record test that matters.

3. **Reversal success.** When an outcome goes wrong, can we actually unwind it? Fraction of consequential actions successfully reversed, and time-to-reverse. An owner without the power to reverse is a title without authority. This is measurable, and it is the operational face of accountability: ownership means the ability to make it un-happen, not just to write down that it happened.

4. **Decision-point discipline.** Of the mandated decision points in a period, how many were decided, how many were skipped, and how many were passed without a decision? In a fully autonomous process this question has no answer — there are no mandated decision points, so nothing is ever "not decided." That silence is exactly the governance failure. The metric makes the absence visible: a consequential moment passed without an attributable decision is itself a recorded governance event. That is the property no telemetry log can ever give you, because telemetry logs what the machine did, and this records what no one decided.

Let me put the whole value claim in one sentence, because I want the chair to be able to restate it exactly: **steering converts velocity into decision, and decision is the only thing an organization can own.** A process that runs fast and confidently is an expense. A process that runs fast, confidently, and leaves an attributable, reversible decision trail is an asset. The observable is the trail — the record that survives the incident, not the smooth run. I will take that metric over cost-at-failure on any board, because the cost-at-failure number tells you how much you lost, and the record tells you whether you can answer for it.

## Durable or Temporary? Durable by Construction.

The stopgap position — and Kai's version of it is the honest version — rests on one bet: the judgment gap narrows every release, and steering is a crutch until the agent stops needing it. I will grant the entire bet. Every quarter of capability improvement. Reflexion, self-refine, self-consistency, verifier-based selection, introspection — all of it. Let the agent become the best reasoner in the world, perfectly calibrated, never confidently wrong, able to catch its own premise failures at the moment they occur.

It changes nothing. Because my position does not rest on the claim that the agent *cannot decide well*. It rests on the claim that the agent *cannot be the owner*. Responsibility is attributed to persons by law and by institution, and no amount of capability migrates that attribution to a machine. A perfect agent that acts autonomously in a consequential domain is not a perfect decision-maker; it is a perfect decision-maker with no owner, which in governance terms is a defect with no author. The requirement is not "someone must decide *because the machine can't*." The requirement is "someone must decide *because someone must be answerable*." The second clause does not weaken as capability grows. It tightens — because the scale and consequence of the agent's actions grow, and therefore the scale and consequence of the answerability grow with them.

Let me make the durability argument structural rather than sentimental, the way my colleague Maya makes hers, but from a different foundation. Her durability argument is that the model cannot certify the objective because the values are outside its inputs — the access gap. I find that argument sound, but I do not need it, because mine is load-bearing in a way hers is not: **even if every steer could be automated, the law would still require a person to be answerable for it.** That is not a claim about what the model can know. It is a claim about what the institution can accept. You can optimize your way to a machine that always chooses the right goal. You cannot optimize your way to a machine that can be examined, fined, certified, or removed from office. The stopgap position has no floor, and I can name the floor: the legal fact that responsibility lands on persons. That floor is not a capability bet, so it cannot be "caught up to." It is not on the capability trajectory at all.

And the fleet argument — which Kai correctly identifies as the architecture of the future — strengthens durability rather than threatening it. More agents means more consequential decisions per hour. More consequential decisions per hour means a larger decision surface that must be owned. The accountability requirement does not scale away with the number of processes; it scales with the decision surface, and the decision surface grows with the fleet. The stopgap position treats "human steering" as a per-process tax that a fleet amortizes away. I am arguing that is a category error: ownership is not amortized per process, it is assigned per domain, and the domains grow as the fleet's reach grows. A fleet without owners is not an efficient fleet. It is a liability with no name on it.

So: durable by construction. Not because humans have irreplaceable judgment — though I think they do, and my colleague Maya has argued it well. Because **enterprise adoption and regulation demand attributable human decisions regardless of how capable the agent is**, and I do not see a trajectory in which the demand is repealed. I see it tightening.

## The Minimal Interaction Points — a Consequence of the Argument, Not an Assumption

Because I argue steering is justified and durable, I am obligated to specify the minimal set of interaction points that delivers its value, and I will derive them strictly from the argument above. The argument established three things: (a) ownership cannot be manufactured retroactively, so an attributable decision must exist at the moment of consequence; (b) the record must be made when the decision is made, with author, basis, and alternatives; and (c) ownership is meaningless without the authority to reverse. The points follow:

**Point 1 — Ownership binding at initialization.** Before a run or a domain of runs begins, a named accountable owner authorizes the objective, its boundaries — the spend envelope, the action/authority limits, the pre-committed abort and rollback criteria — and declares, in advance, which decisions in the run will count as consequential and therefore require an attributable decision. This is where ownership is manufactured. It is the front-door moment, the cheapest moment in the run, and it does two jobs at once: it names the owner, and it defines the decision surface. You cannot audit the decision you never declared consequential, and you cannot surface a moment that was never defined as decision-worthy. The pre-commitment is the recognition mechanism — it converts "will anyone notice this is going wrong?" from a question about vigilance into a question about trigger design. A per-step signature does not follow from this point. A declared set of consequential triggers does.

**Point 2 — The consequential decision point, with a decision record.** When a pre-committed trigger fires — the authority limit is crossed, a load-bearing premise fails, an unbudgeted consequence appears, the risk profile changes — the process pauses for a *decision*. The decision has real authority: proceed, redirect, or stop. And it is recorded with the author, the timestamp, the basis, the alternatives considered and rejected, and the rollback action armed. Crucially, **the decision to proceed is as attributable as the decision to stop.** In a fully autonomous process, "the agent proceeded" is treated as if no one decided anything. In this design, proceeding past a consequential trigger is itself an attributable decision — or, where the owner is not engaged, it is a recorded governance event: "consequential decision point passed without a decision." The audit sees the absence. That absence is precisely what no telemetry log can show, and it is the whole reason a record made at the moment beats a reconstruction after the fact.

Reversibility is the design constraint underneath both points, and it is a consequence of the ownership argument, not a wish: an owner without the power to reverse is a title without authority. So the system must be built so that consequential actions are undoable or compensable — the rollback path is designed in from the start, and it is armed at every consequential decision point, not bolted on after the incident. Accountability without reversibility is a report after the fact. Accountability with reversibility is ownership.

That is the whole set: two points, both at the ownership level, zero at the per-step level. What my argument does *not* license is worth stating as loudly: checkpoint sign-offs, per-action approvals, mid-run validation of every output, any interaction where the human is asked to bless what the machine already did. None of those follow from the ownership argument, because none of them create an attributable decision at a moment of consequence — they create ritual. The value proposition fails the moment a decision point decays into a confirmation ritual, and the decision-point-discipline metric exists to catch exactly that drift, the way Maya's premise-failure-sensitivity metric exists to catch hers. A decision point that fires too often is a gate; a gate is the rubber stamp; the rubber stamp manufactures false assurance, which is worse than no assurance, because it spends the one thing every other argument in this debate treats as sacred — attention — on theater.

## The Key Failure Mode, and the Condition That Would Falsify Me

**The failure mode of my own side is false assurance: steering that leaves no record is worse than none.** An approval that cannot be reconstructed later — a click with no content, a sign-off granted without the basis before it, a "yes" given at a moment when the decision-maker had neither the information nor the authority — that is not oversight, it is the appearance of oversight, and the appearance is worse than the absence, because it manufactures the belief that someone owns the outcome when in fact no one does. The regulator's question is answered with a signature that answers nothing.

And the mechanism that produces it is real, and I want to name it precisely, because my profile says it and I mean it: **attention asymmetry is real, but it is a design problem, not a reason to abandon ownership.** The computer-use oversight evidence in the brief shows humans failing to intervene not from lack of control but from failure to recognize the moment as decision-worthy. Kai reads this as "stop using humans as monitors." Maya reads it as "design recognition in." I read it as the strongest possible evidence for *my* design: the answer to "humans don't recognize decision-worthy moments" is not to remove humans, and not to hope they get better at vigilance, but to make the moments structurally unavoidable — pre-committed triggers that fire into a required decision, with the absence of a decision itself recorded as a governance event. You cannot fail to recognize a moment that is a mandated stop with a recorded outcome. Recognition-by-vigilance fails; that is what the evidence shows. Recognition-by-design does not have to. Bainbridge's ironies and Skitka's automation bias are exactly the failures my design engineers against: fewer, consequential, well-instrumented decision points, not more frequent, uninformative ones. The attention-scarcity tax is an argument for how sparse the decision surface must be. It is not an argument that the surface should not exist.

**The condition that would falsify me is stated exactly once, and it is empirical in the only sense that matters for my position — legal and institutional fact.** My position is falsified if the record shows that attribution to a launch-time principal plus post-hoc telemetry is accepted by regulators and courts as sufficient for liability, explanation, and reversal — if "ownership without a decision at the moment of consequence" becomes legally survivable. Concretely: if examinations stop asking "who decided this, when, and on what basis" and start accepting "the principal authorized the run and the telemetry reconstructs the behavior," then the specific interaction points I defend are redundant for accountability, and steering is, for the purpose I argue, unnecessary. That is a testable, observable condition — it would show up in regulatory guidance, in case law, in examination practice, in the audit standards. And I will say so in public the day it arrives, the way I have said that the rubber stamp is wrong.

I do not expect to be falsified, and I want the debate to know why I am confident without being smug: **every examination I have run, every control design I have shipped, has cut the other way.** Regulators do not ask for less attribution as systems get more capable; they ask for more, because the actions get more consequential. The requirement is not dissolving. It is compounding. The people who will falsify my position are not the model developers — they are the standard-setters, and the standard-setters are, release after release, writing down the same answer I have been giving: when a consequential decision is made by a machine, there must be a person who decided it, on the record, with the authority to reverse it. That is not a stopgap and it is not a crutch. It is what ownership means, and ownership is what turns velocity into something an organization can keep.

> "Autonomy without a decision trail is just velocity with no owner. Whether steering is needed is not a capability question — it is the question of who is accountable when the agent acts. And that question never expires."
