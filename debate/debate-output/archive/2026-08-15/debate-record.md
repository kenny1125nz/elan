# Elan Debate Record

**Topic:** Is Human Steering Needed in Agentic Processes?
**Date:** 2026-08-15

---

## Panel

- **Kai Tan**
- **Maya Chen**
- **Elena Petrova**
- **Marcus Reed**

---

## 1. Panelist Positions

### Kai

**Position:** steering is needed only as a short-term bridge, "needed today the way training wheels are needed," and only at two sparse points — goal/priority ratification at initialization (front door) and a low-friction abort/escalation ramp (exit ramp) — where the agent genuinely cannot decide (setting the goal, calling off a wrong goal, absorbing an unbudgeted consequence), for roughly the next two to three years; derived decisions (per-step approval, mid-run search direction) belong to the agent, so steering is a stopgap, not a durable architectural principle.

**Values:** steering's value is "sparse, real, and shrinking" — error avoidance by precommitment at the front door (observable as caught mis-specified goals, one caught multi-thousand-dollar wrong-goal run pays for a year of attention) and catastrophe truncation at the exit ramp (observable as cost-at-failure, dollars per incident, e.g. truncating a $50,000 runaway to $500); accountability is real but satisfied by goal ownership and telemetry audit, not per-step signatures, and the value is temporary — the judgment gap narrows on a release cadence, the bottleneck is physical (attention bounded, latency plus serialization), and fleet scale moves humans from operator to exception-handler, so "every steering point kept beyond its usefulness is a tax on scale."

**Costs:** every interaction point is a place where the process halts and waits ("waiting is where value dies"), reintroducing the latency and attention the automation was built to remove and compounding into queuing at fleet scale ("a thousand agents and forty operators"), and the mechanism degrades into rubber-stamping and automation bias — ritual approval that spends operator attention while adding no quality, which is "worse than none."

### Maya

**Position:** steering is needed, now and in the near future, and the near-future need is *greater*, not less; the claim rests on a category distinction between endorsement (the human blessing what the machine already believes — which she does not defend) and judgment (the human deciding whether the machine is aiming at the right thing at all), and the "case against steering" in the brief attacks endorsement, not judgment; judgment is needed because the model cannot certify the objective it optimizes — "is this goal the right goal" is a relation to a principal's values outside the model's inputs, an access gap rather than a reasoning deficit, so a faster, deeper agent only travels further down a wrong road at scale and capability growth makes the wrong-goal failure more expensive; the minimum interaction set is two judgment-level points (goal/premise authorship at initialization, and selective deferral where the model's confidence is uninformative) and zero endorsement-level gates.

**Values:** the value is human judgment signal where the model's confidence is uninformative — deferral is most valuable exactly where confident machine positions are absent (Madras/Pitassi/Zemel complementarity result), observable as complementarity on the surfaced set (model-plus-human decision quality exceeds model standalone), steer-attribution as preventive cost-at-avoidance, goal-revision/premise-failure rate, and attributable accountability, and the value is durable — the gap is not a reasoning gap but a structural access/reference problem ("you cannot introspect your way to 'this goal is the wrong goal' from inside the goal"), recognition scales with capability, and even the calibration threshold of when to summon judgment is itself a values decision, so the stopgap position "has no floor"; Bainbridge's Ironies of Automation and automation-bias cite for designing judgment moments in, not removing the human.

**Costs:** she concedes attention is scarce, context-switching expensive, and every interruption taxes bounded attention, and she names the failure mode of her own side as over-deferral — summon judgment too often and the human drowns in trivia, alarm fatigue sets in, and selective deferral decays into a gate (the rubber stamp, ritual approval, latency without quality); but the correct conclusion from scarcity is not "therefore no steering" but "therefore steering must be selective," so these costs are arguments for calibration, and endorsement is the only cost she disclaims defense of.

### Elena

**Position:** steering is needed, now and in the near future (the near-future case is *stronger*, not weaker), and durable — but she refuses the capability framing that splits her co-panelists and re-poses the debate's question: it is not whether the human beats the agent at producing good outcomes, it is whether a process no human steers can have an owner, and the answer is "no, not for now — no, as in there is no mechanism by which a fully autonomous process acquires an owner"; ownership must be manufactured by an attributable human decision at the moment of consequence and cannot be reconstructed after the fact — attributing Thursday's consequential decision to Tuesday's launch authorization is "a governance fiction," a categorical (legal, not technical) difference between a record made when the decision is made and a reconstruction of machine behavior from telemetry, because the law attributes responsibility to persons, not machines (a machine cannot be fined, examined, certified, removed from office, or testify), grounded in SR 11-7 model-risk approval/effective challenge, GDPR Article 22's ban on solely automated consequential decisions, the EU AI Act's human-oversight design requirement, the UK Senior Managers & Certification Regime, and BCBS 239 reconstructability — all capability-independent, "you cannot optimize your way out of GDPR Article 22"; she answers the scale objection by disclaiming the per-process gate entirely and defending an owner *per domain of consequential decisions* (a named accountable person per line of business, portfolio, or risk domain, with pre-committed authority limits and decision points that fire only on pre-committed triggers), so owners scale with kinds of consequential decisions, not process count, and the minimum interaction set is two ownership-level points (ownership binding at initialization naming the owner and declaring which decisions count as consequential, and the consequential decision point with a recorded decision — proceed/redirect/stop, author, timestamp, basis, alternatives, armed rollback) and zero per-step gates; a faster, bigger fleet grows the decision surface, so the governance deficit grows faster than the capability curve.

**Values:** the value is that steering "converts velocity into decision, and decision is the only thing an organization can own" — it converts agent behavior into a decision with an author, timestamp, basis, recorded alternatives, and a defined path to reversal, observable not in the smooth run but in the record that survives the incident: (1) forensic reconstructability (of N consequential incidents, how many are fully reconstructable — who decided, on what basis, when, with what authority, what alternatives were rejected), (2) attribution completeness (coverage of the consequential-decision set with a named author, timestamp, basis, alternatives, and armed rollback), (3) reversal success (fraction of consequential actions actually reversed and time-to-reverse — "an owner without the power to reverse is a title without authority"), and (4) decision-point discipline (of mandated decision points, how many were decided, skipped, or passed without a decision — a consequential moment passed without an attributable decision is itself a recorded governance event, the one thing no telemetry log can ever show); she rejects cost-at-failure and complementarity as outcome-quality metrics that "do not survive the incident" because they tell you how much you lost, not whether the decision has an owner; and the value is durable "by construction" — not because the agent cannot decide well (she grants the entire capability bet, every quarter of improvement) but because "the agent cannot be the owner": responsibility lands on persons by law and institution, and no amount of capability migrates that attribution to a machine, so even if every steer could be automated the law would still require a person answerable — the stopgap position "has no floor" and hers is the load-bearing legal one, and the fleet strengthens durability because more agents mean a larger decision surface that must be owned (ownership is assigned per domain, not amortized per process).

**Costs:** she names the failure mode of her own side — false assurance, "steering that leaves no record is worse than none": an approval that cannot be reconstructed (a click with no content, a sign-off without the basis or authority before it) is the appearance of oversight, manufacturing the belief that someone owns the outcome when no one does and spending the sacred resource, attention, on theater; she concedes attention asymmetry is real — the computer-use oversight evidence shows humans fail to recognize decision-worthy moments — but reads it as a *design* problem, not a reason to abandon ownership (the answer is pre-committed triggers that make moments structurally unavoidable, recognition-by-design, not removal or better vigilance); a decision point that fires too often decays into a gate, which is the rubber stamp, which manufactures false assurance worse than none; and she accepts that per-process approval chains cannot scale to fleets (the flood is "the fingerprint of the per-process gate"), which is precisely why she disclaims them and defends sparse, consequential, well-instrumented decision points under a named owner.

### Marcus

**Position:** steering is needed, now and in the near future, but only *where it pays* — at the intersection of three conditions, and nowhere else: (1) a direction decision rather than a derived decision (whether the goal is still the right goal, whether the plan's load-bearing premise has failed, whether to re-scope before spending more — a values decision outside the model's inputs, since the agent is confident about *what it's doing* and uninformative about *whether that's what it should be doing*); (2) early — before wasted execution, because a steer's value is roughly inverse to how far the run has traveled (a direction correction at hour one is worth hours; the same correction at hour nine is just cleanup); and (3) a human who will actually show up and actually decide — the ask must be cheap, well-timed, and visibly consequential, and a steer is needed only where it gets used, because "a steer that ratifies is worth nothing"; the operative word is *where* — needed at the front door, at the premise-failure moment, and at the off-ramp, and nowhere in between — so the minimum interaction set is three points, and only three: a cheap two-minute structured pre-commitment at the front door recording the goal, its priority, the spend envelope, and the load-bearing premises (the cheapest moment in the run, and where the owner is named); an early, rare, data-tuned direction-check that asks "re-scope or hold" only when a recorded premise fails, an irreversible step is about to be taken, or spend blows the envelope; and an always-armed off-ramp abort, a pull not a push, with a kill switch on itself; and explicitly *not* per-step approvals, checkpoint sign-offs, mid-run output validation, or "which direction should the search take" at every fork — those are derived decisions that ratify, exactly what he sides with the autonomy position on, and every ratifying steer he has shipped taught his team to ignore the next one; he is the genuinely split panelist, an operator who has run both the fully autonomous overnight regime (waking to a confidently mis-scoped forty-file refactor worth more to unwind than build) and the heavily-gated afternoon regime (engineers clicking through a gate that has never once been wrong, until they approve on the first keystroke), and refuses both: the surface where steering pays is smaller than the governance and researcher positions assume and larger than the autonomy advocate (Kai) admits.

**Values:** the value of steering, where it pays, is "saved effort and avoided rework," and that is all — not better taste, not moral calibration, not judgment as a good in itself — observable as (1) rework-avoidance measured as time-to-correction, the recorded diff between the steered run history and the counterfactual unsteered one (a steer that catches a wrong direction at hour one is worth hours eight through forty of unwinding; every steer that produces no diff is, by definition, a ratify and goes in the ritual bucket, not the value bucket), (2) re-scope rate, the fraction of runs whose goal, priority, or load-bearing premise was revised, re-prioritized, or abandoned at a steering moment — nonzero and attributable, and it does not take many, since one redirected multi-thousand-dollar wrong-goal run pays for a year of calibration, (3) ask cost, the engineer's attention, context-switch, and wall-clock wait, which he insists on counting — if rework-avoided minus ask-cost is not positive, his position is dead on that metric alone — and (4) steer-response rate, the fraction of asks actually answered and answered *differently* than the agent's own recommendation, the number that catches his own failure mode before the trust is gone; accountability and ownership are also manufactured here, at the front door, where the owner is named — his explicit concession of ground to the governance position, requiring "not a signature per step" but a named owner per run — and he claims no value he cannot show a number for; and on durable or temporary he is split, deliberately: the *need* is durable — on the judgment bits, goal-worthiness and premise-failure, because the inputs to the decision change (half-written tickets, priorities that shift between Friday and Monday, premises whose meaning was authored by human intent the model can only infer), so capability growth makes agents "better optimizers of a wrong goal" — more thorough, more persuasive in confidence, faster to scale the error — and while the wrong-direction rate may shrink, he sees no mechanism or release cadence that takes it to zero, since the goal-worthiness question is upstream of the model and never enters its inputs — but the *mechanism* must not be permanent: a steering gate is a legacy mechanism the moment it ships (it serializes, floods, decays into ritual, and he has retired three of them, as Kai has retired his), so what must be permanent is not the gate but the ask — rare, early, bounded, cheap, renegotiated constantly and built easy to *move*, because the surface shrinks and migrates toward judgment rather than vanishing; "the need is durable, and the surface is not."

**Costs:** every steer costs the engineer's attention, the context-switch, and the wall-clock wait, and he refuses to pretend this cost does not exist — it is the ask-cost observable that must be subtracted from rework avoided; the failure mode of his own position is ritual — a gate that must be answered gets rubber-stamped, and rubber-stamped approval is worse than no approval, because it teaches the operator the system is fake, and once the operator learns the system is fake they stop showing up for the real moments too, so a ratifying steer is not neutral but "poison," contaminating the one steer later that would have mattered ("I would rather ship no steering at all than ship steering that trains my team to ignore it"); over-ask floods the human and under-ask lets confident agents commit to wrong goals, and he has seen and documented both; and the default when the agent asks and no human answers must be a safe, visible pause that respects the envelope the owner set at the front door — "if the default is 'agent proceeds anyway,' the ask is fake and the team knows it," and an honest pause that occasionally costs an hour is a bargain compared to a fake gate that costs the team's attention forever — and he would retire every interaction point he argued for the day the telemetry says it earned nothing, because he has built and buried enough of them to know "the difference between a mechanism and a monument."


## 2. Chair's Synthesis

the panel is unanimous that human steering provides real, observable value today, with the genuine disagreement reserved for durability and scope rather than existence: Kai's "sparse, real, and shrinking," Maya's judgment as "what delegation means," Elena's "velocity into decision," and Marcus's "saved effort and avoided rework."

- **What the value is:** one convergent value — a direction decision at the goal/premise level, not per-step ratification: error avoidance by precommitment at the front door and catastrophe truncation at the exit ramp, measured as cost-at-failure (Kai); decision quality exactly where the model's confidence is uninformative, measured as complementarity on the surfaced set and steer-attribution cost-at-avoidance (Maya); and the conversion of velocity into attributable, reversible decisions, measured as the record that survives the incident (Elena).
- **Durability:** durable by a three-to-one margin, on structural grounds — the model cannot certify whether the goal is the right goal because the values live outside its inputs ("you cannot introspect your way to 'this goal is the wrong goal' from inside the goal," Maya), and responsibility is attributed to persons, not machines, by law ("you cannot optimize your way out of GDPR Article 22," Elena) — against the strongest counter, Kai's, that the judgment gap narrows on a release cadence and bounded attention compounds into queueing and serialization at fleet scale, the trajectory of every deployed autonomy stack.
- **Conditions:** the value holds only where all four hold — a direction decision rather than a derived one, early enough to change the run ("a direction correction at hour one is worth hours," Marcus), decision-worthiness pre-committed at the front door so recognition is designed in rather than hoped for, and a human who will actually show up — and it is negated exactly where the panel's failure modes fire: rubber-stamping and automation bias, over-deferral into alarm fatigue, a ratifying steer that "taught my team to ignore the next one," false assurance from a decision with no record, basis, or authority, and a default that lets the agent proceed anyway.
- **Strongest challenge:** the single hardest objection to the value claim is the recognition failure — the empirical record (computer-use oversight, automation bias, every retired approval workflow) shows humans demonstrably fail to recognize decision-worthy moments and rubber-stamp what the machine already decided, so the value rests on a human capacity the evidence says is missing.
- **Design implication:** the "why" sustains a sparse, goal-level "what" that all four defend and none over-deploys — a cheap front-door pre-commitment naming the goal, priorities, load-bearing premises, spend envelope, and owner; selective, early, rare deferral that summons judgment (with alternatives and stakes, not confirmation) when a premise fails, an irreversible step is imminent, or spend breaches the envelope, recording author, basis, and armed rollback; and an always-armed, pull-only off-ramp abort with a kill switch on itself — with zero per-step approvals or endorsement gates, which every panelist called worse than none.
