## 1. Panelist Positions

### Kai Tan (Autonomy Advocate)

- **Position:** Steering is needed only at two narrow, non-derivable moments — a terminal goal check and the irreversible-action gate — but most per-step steering is a delay with a person attached, and he argues mid-run steering will be overtaken as agent capability improves.
- **Values:** Human steering provides durable value only as a terminal goal check (desirability is not derivable from agent reasoning and survives capability gains) and as attribution for irreversible or accountability-bearing actions that no capability improvement can replace; he treats all other steering value — recognition, mid-run correction, dense oversight — as temporary, with its compensation shrinking below the cost of the gate.
- **Costs:** The costs are measurable latency added per steer, attention consumed per steer, and steers that change nothing — evidenced by override rates where people were rubber-stamping checkpoints the machine had already passed — plus the fatal scale arithmetic that a human per process is an architectural dead end for fleets of agents.

### Dr. Maya Chen (Human Judgment Researcher)

- **Position:** Steering is needed now and in the near future, because its core is judgment — choosing the objective, revising it, and deciding when it is no longer worth pursuing — which is not a reasoning ability on the capability curve; she concedes the endorsement form of steering is a stopgap that should be absorbed.
- **Values:** The value is judgment, concentrated at exactly the moments where the model's own features are uninformative (goal-setting, goal-revision, and direction-setting under ambiguity), and she argues it is durable — steering concentrates, not disappears, as capability grows — because the objective-choice is a delegation about the machine's goal, not a computation within it, with the residual human-added signal measurable by a held-out complementarity test.
- **Costs:** The cost is any interaction point built around frequency and endorsement — rubber-stamping, monitoring, and vigilance failure — which she says adds latency and attention without quality and should be designed out; she also concedes that a steering mechanism that lacks consequentiality (the gate cannot stop the process) dies as theater, and that the practical case weakens if the 2026 failures were control failures rather than recognition failures.

### Elena Petrova (Governance & Accountability Lead)

- **Position:** Steering is needed now and in the near future — not for decision quality, which she concedes the engineers can win honestly, but because a process no one steers has no owner, and the law (GDPR Art. 22, EU AI Act oversight, SR 11-7 effective challenge, SM&CR, BCBS 239) does not recognize ownerless consequential action.
- **Values:** The value is the manufacture of an owner — attribution, explanation under challenge, reversal, and regulatory standing: a reconstructable, challengeable decision trail that ends in a named person, observable after an incident; she argues this value is durable and capability-independent because ownership is a legal status, not a threshold of intelligence, and capability gains strengthen rather than erode it (more capable agents take more consequential, harder-to-reverse actions), being falsifiable only by a change in law, not a change in model capability.
- **Costs:** The negative side is false assurance — steering that degrades into ceremony (mandates signed unread, escalations ignored, reaffirmation rubber-stamped) adds latency while adding no ownership and is strictly worse than no steering because it manufactures the appearance of accountability; she also counts per-step approval gates as a cost, since they flood, fail, and turn the human from judge into notary.

### Marcus Reed (Operating Engineer)

- **Position:** Steering is needed now and stays needed in the near future — but only as direction-setting (the goal set-and-confirm and the event-triggered drift interrupt); every derived, ratifiable decision (plan approval, per-step gates) is a stopgap capability will and should absorb.
- **Values:** The durable value is judgment at direction moments — the human owning whether a goal is worth pursuing, an authority decision rather than a capability problem, so the interaction survives and its surface area shrinks instead of disappearing; it is observable via redirected-before-execution rate, human-disagrees-with-agent rate, and rework avoided net of wait cost.
- **Costs:** Ratification is not merely worthless but corrosive — it trains people to rubber-stamp, pushing approval toward 100% and attention toward zero and burning a busy engineer's attention and trust — while bottleneck latency, context-switching, and a human-per-process scale dead end are real costs, but only of the derived-class steering he argues should be deleted.

## 2. Chair's Synthesis

1. **Value verdict:** The panel converges that human steering provides real, observable value today — all four members, including the strongest skeptic, defend a durable core — but only at a narrow, consequential set of moments, not across the process.
2. **What the value is:** The panel identified two concrete values in its own terms — judgment at direction moments, the human's decision that a goal is worth pursuing (Kai's "desirability is not derivable," Maya's "delegation about the machine's goal," Marcus's "authority decision"), and the manufacture of an owner, a challengeable decision trail that ends in a named person (Elena's ownership, Kai's attribution for irreversible actions).
3. **Durability:** The value is durable where it sits off the capability curve — desirability, judgment, and ownership are delegations and legal statuses, not computations, so capability gains concentrate rather than erase them — yet Kai and Maya both concede the mid-run, per-step forms are a stopgap that capability will absorb, leaving durability only at the narrowing surface of direction-setting.
4. **Conditions:** The value holds only when the interaction is consequential (the gate can actually stop the process — Maya), sits at direction and terminal moments rather than per-step endorsement (Kai, Marcus), and manufactures genuine ownership rather than ceremony (Elena) — and it fails exactly when rubber-stamping, false assurance, or the conflation of control failures with recognition failures takes over.
5. **Strongest challenge:** The single hardest objection is Elena's false-assurance argument — steering that degrades into ceremony is not merely valueless but strictly worse than no steering because it manufactures the appearance of accountability — and every panelist conceded the mechanism's internal drift toward rubber-stamping makes that degradation the default.
6. **Design implication:** The 'why' supports a minimal, consequential 'what' — a goal set-and-confirm as the terminal goal check, a consequential gate on irreversible or accountability-bearing actions, and an event-triggered drift interrupt for goal revision — while ruling out per-step approval, endorsement, and frequency-based monitoring as derived stopgaps.
