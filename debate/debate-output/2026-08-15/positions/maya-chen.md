# Position: Steering Is Needed, and Its Core Value Is Durable

**Dr. Maya Chen — Human Judgment Researcher, Human-AI Collaboration (HCI)**

I am going to make a single analytical cut, and then argue that everything in this debate turns on it. The cut is between **judgment** and **endorsement**. The case *against* steering — Claim B — is an attack on endorsement, and much of it is correct. The case *for* steering — Claim A — is really a claim about judgment, and it is the part Claim B never touches. My position: human steering is needed now and in the near future, its value is the value of judgment, and that value is not temporary. Not because agents are stupid — I am not arguing that — but because judgment is not a reasoning ability, so it is not on the capability curve. I will argue this honestly, and I will tell you exactly what would falsify it.

---

## 1. The distinction the debate keeps eliding

The brief asks whether human steering is needed. But there are two radically different things that get called "steering":

**Endorsement** — a human approves or rejects a decision the machine has already formed. This is a gate that asks the human to ratify what the machine already believes. It adds latency, costs attention, and tends to produce exactly the rubber-stamping the brief warns about. Endorsement is the natural target of the stopgap argument, and I will not defend it. I have spent a career watching gates like these. When the model's confidence is well-calibrated and its competence high, endorsement-shaped interaction points are pure overhead, and they degrade into ritual approval, worse than none.

**Judgment** — the expression of a principal's values in a delegation. Priorities. Whether a goal is still worth pursuing. Which direction the search should take when the objective itself is in question. This is not a verdict on the machine's output. It is an input the machine cannot generate, because it is *about* the machine's objective, not *within* it.

Claim B says agents get better at reasoning, planning, and self-correction, therefore steering is a crutch. That is a true sentence about endorsement and a false one about judgment. Reasoning, planning, and self-correction are abilities. They get better every quarter. Judgment — deciding that a goal is the right goal, or that a goal we set last quarter is no longer worth pursuing — is not an ability that improves along that curve, because it is not a computation over the inputs. It is a values decision about which inputs should exist.

This is why my lab's finding keeps repeating across domains. In medical triage, in code review, in content moderation, the value of the human input is concentrated in judgment. The endorsement the human contributes — "yes, the model's diagnosis looks right" — is redundant with the model. The judgment — "this patient's stated preferences should override the survival-maximizing protocol" — is not redundant with anything the model has.

## 2. The prior art says the value lives where the model cannot self-assess

The selective-prediction literature — Chow's rejection option, El-Yaniv & Wiener's risk-based abstention — gives us the precise statement. A model may abstain, and abstention pays off exactly when the model's confidence is uninformative. But note what that means. If a model's confidence *reliably* told you when it was wrong, you would not need a human at all — you would have perfect selective prediction, with the model rejecting precisely the cases it would have gotten wrong, and you could fix them by any means. The empirical fact that this does not close is not a curiosity; it is the definition of the case where deferral is valuable. The cases worth deferring are, by construction, cases the model cannot distinguish from the cases it handles fine.

Madras, Pitassi & Zemel (2018) and Mozannar & Sontag (2020) made this concrete: you can *learn* the deferral policy, routing cases to a human expert. But the routing rule only helps when the model's own signal stops being an oracle. The moment the model could know which cases need a human, it could just do better on them itself, and the deferral would evaporate. That is the equilibrium the literature keeps hitting: **human input retains value precisely in the residual where the model's features cannot discriminate, and that residual does not vanish because a model cannot, in general, identify the cases its own features cannot separate.** What looks like a temporary crutch is, structurally, a permanent complement.

And this is exactly what makes the value *observable*. The complementarity result — that human-AI teams outperform either alone — is not a slogan. It has a measurement: hold out the deferred decision mass, and evaluate three conditions — model alone, human alone, model-plus-human — on the cases routed to the human. If the human's signal improves decisions on cases the model cannot discriminate from its features, the value is real and it is measurable in exactly the place Claim B says there is nothing. If the routing mass shrinks over time as capability grows, fine — that is the endorsement component being absorbed, and I expect it to shrink. But the measured error reduction on the residual mass is the observable value, and it is observable specifically on the cases where the model's confidence is uninformative.

## 3. The goal-worthiness question is outside the inputs, and that is not a temporary fact

Claim B's strongest form is: capabilities improve, therefore the judgment gap narrows every release, therefore designing steering in now is betting on a moving target. I want to concede what is true in this. A model that cannot plan, cannot correct its own reasoning, or hallucinates constantly requires steering for reasons of *competence*. That steering is a stopgap. Fix the competence and that kind of steering disappears, as it should.

But the goal-worthiness question is a different kind of question. It cannot enter the model's inputs as a fact, because if it could, it would be a fact and no one would need to judge it. "Is this goal still worth pursuing?" is not derivable from the goal's own specification — it is a statement about the world, about resources, about risk tolerance, about the principal's changing values. You can wire a preference function in. You can try to elicit the principal's values into a rubric. What you cannot do is make the model *the author* of the decision about whether its own objective is the right one, because that decision is not a computation on the objective. It is a delegation. Mixed-initiative theory (Allen 1999; Horvitz 1999) has said the same thing in slightly different vocabulary for decades: authority and initiative are shared along a sliding scale, and the reason we slide rather than collapse the scale to zero is that initiative without a principal's authorization is no longer the principal's decision.

This is not a claim about model size or training data. It is a claim about where an objective comes from. Models are instruments for optimizing an objective. Human steering is the activity of *choosing the objective*, revising it, and deciding when it is no longer worth optimizing. Instrument quality improves forever. The choice of objective does not improve with instrument quality, because it is not an instrument competency.

So here is the honest formulation of my durability claim: **steering does not disappear as capability grows — it concentrates.** The mechanical component of steering — correction, format, hallucination-catching, most endorsement — gets absorbed into the model. What remains is the judgment component, and because that component was never a competence gap, it does not close. The fraction of total interaction that is pure judgment *rises* as the mechanical fraction falls. Steering becomes rarer, more concentrated, and more consequential. That is the opposite of the stopgap story. The stopgap story predicts steering becomes a smaller and smaller contributor until it is noise; the judgment story predicts the interaction points become fewer and heavier until all that remains is the delegation itself.

## 4. The attention objection is an objection to bad steering, not to steering

Claim B says every interaction point is a place where the process waits on a human, and that bounded attention does not scale to fleets. This is the strongest practical objection and I want to meet it directly, because the recognition evidence actually disarms it.

The computer-use oversight evidence from 2026 is specific and uncomfortable for both sides. Humans in those studies failed to intervene not because they lacked control — they had plenty of control — but because they did not *recognize* the moment as decision-worthy. They watched a process doing something wrong and did not notice that a decision was being made. That finding kills the naive version of Claim A — the "a human must watch every step" version, which is monitoring, and monitoring is the single worst job you can give a human. Bainbridge's Ironies of Automation told us this in 1983: when you reduce the human to a monitor, you produce exactly the vigilance failure that then gets blamed on the human. Automation bias does the rest — people approve what the machine decided, which is endorsement, which is the thing I am not defending.

But the same finding disarms Claim B. If the failure were lack of control, then the answer to "should we have humans steering thousands of processes" would be "no, we cannot staff that." The evidence says the failure is recognition: humans had control and did not know when to use it. That is a *design* failure of the steering mechanism — the decision-worthy moments were not made visible — not a proof that no such moments exist. Steering that works does not ask often. It asks rarely, and it makes the rare moments unmissable. The bottleneck objection is a critique of interaction points built around frequency and endorsement. It is not a critique of interaction points built around recognition and judgment. When the cost of a steer is one deliberate decision a week on a fleet that would otherwise run on, the "bounded attention" objection collapses.

Scale follows from the same logic. A human per process is an architectural dead end — agreed, and irrelevant. Judgment does not require a human per process. It requires that the fleet surface decision-worthy moments and route them to a principal whose values are the ones in force. That is a routing problem, and routing is precisely what the deferral literature says is learnable. The number of humans scales with the number of distinct value-holders, not with the number of processes.

## 5. The minimal interaction points — as a consequence, not an assumption

The brief asks me, if I argue steering is justified, to specify the minimal interaction points *as a consequence of the argument*. Here they are, derived, not assumed.

The value of steering is judgment, and judgment is only needed where the model's features are uninformative. Therefore the interaction points are exactly three, and they are all *rare*:

1. **Goal-setting.** Before a process runs, the principal states what is worth optimizing and why — the values that make the delegation legitimate. One point, at initiation. Without it, there is no principal and no owner; this is also where the accountability value lives, and it is observable in the audit record: an attributable decision that the goal was the right goal.

2. **Goal-revision triggers.** The model routes to the principal when a decision-worthy moment arises — the goal's premises have been violated, the cost has crossed a threshold, the world has changed such that the objective is in question. This is the deferral condition from the selective-prediction literature: the model asks when its own confidence that the *objective is still worth pursuing* is uninformative, which is exactly when it cannot answer for itself. Notice this is not "ask when uncertain about the answer" — that is endorsement and should be handled by the model. It is "ask when the objective itself is in question," which the model can flag but not decide.

3. **Direction-setting under ambiguity.** When the search space forks and the choice encodes a value — risk tolerance, taste, whose preferences count — the principal steers. This is the mixed-initiative "where should the initiative go" moment, and it is the point my triage, code-review, and moderation findings keep locating: the human adds signal where the model is weak, not where it is strong.

Everything else — every approval of a specific output, every format fix, every correction the model can self-serve — is endorsement, and I would design it *out*. A steering mechanism with only these three points is cheap in attention, rich in judgment, and immune to the rubber-stamp failure, because there is nothing routine left to stamp.

I will add the caveat my colleagues keep pointing out, and they are right: a mechanism that is theoretically optimal but *feels* like theater gets ignored in practice. The recognition failure cuts both ways. If the three interaction points are designed but the principal learns that "no" is never honored or that the revision is never consequential, the mechanism dies, and it dies precisely because the human no longer recognizes the moments as decision-worthy. Consequentiality is not a nice-to-have; it is load-bearing. The gate must be able to stop the process, or it is not a gate, it is a prop.

## 6. What would falsify this

I am a scientist, and I owe the panel the test. My position has a specific falsifier, and I want it on the record so that the debate is about evidence, not temperament.

My position is falsified if judgment proves to be reducible to competence — if the following can be shown empirically:

**There exists a class of agentic processes with real goal-worthiness decisions, and over successive capability improvements, the residual human-added signal on the deferred judgment cases goes to zero — measured by the held-out complementarity test — while the system's behavior on those cases remains aligned with what the principal would have chosen, and principals cannot distinguish the outcomes.**

That is the load-bearing claim, stated precisely: *the deferred decision mass can be absorbed into the model with no loss of alignment.* If a fleet of agents can, over time, correctly decide to abandon or revise a goal on their own — and do so as well as the principal would have — then the goal-worthiness question did enter the inputs after all, it was a reasoning problem, and my distinction collapses into competence. I would then grant Claim B the day. I am not betting against the model's *capability*; I am betting that the absorbing case never closes, because the alignment check at the end of the sentence is itself a judgment — someone has to decide what "aligned with what the principal would have chosen" means, and that someone is a principal.

There is also a partial, softer falsifier, and I offer it because it is the honest one. If the recognition evidence flips — if it turns out that the 2026 failures were actually control failures after all, and humans *could not* intervene even when they recognized the moment — then my design argument weakens: no design of interaction points can help a human who is physically or temporally unable to act. I would still hold the judgment claim, but I would have to concede that the minimal interaction points are not implementable at scale, and the practical case for steering today takes a real hit.

## 7. Conclusion

Claim B is right about endorsement, and I want the panel to credit it: most of what is currently called "human steering" in deployed systems is endorsement, it is a stopgap, and it will — and should — be absorbed as capability grows. But Claim B mistakes the residue for the whole. The residue that is not absorbed is judgment, and judgment is not a competence gap. It is the activity of being the principal — choosing the objective, revising it, and deciding when it is no longer worth pursuing. Better models will not supply that, because it is not a model competency; it is the thing models are delegated *by*. As capability grows, the mechanical shell of steering falls away and the judgment core is left standing — rarer, heavier, more consequential. That is not the trajectory of a stopgap. It is the trajectory of the one thing in the delegation that cannot be automated.

> A gate asks the human to approve what the machine already believes. The question that matters is whether there is a distinct thing — judgment — that delegation genuinely requires, and whether better models can ever supply it. The evidence says the distinct thing is real, it is where the value concentrates, and it is not on the capability curve. If someone can show me the day it gets absorbed without loss of alignment, I will update. Until then: design for the judgment, and design the endorsement out.
