# Debate Brief: Is Human Steering Needed in Agentic Processes?

## The Question

This debate is about the **why**, not the how. Two competing claims:

> **Claim A — Steering is needed.** Agentic processes act relentlessly and fast. Left unsupervised, they optimize for the wrong goal confidently, at scale, before anyone notices. A human who supplies judgment — priorities, whether a goal is still worth pursuing, which direction the search should take — is the difference between useful delegation and uncontrolled automation.

> **Claim B — Steering is a stopgap.** Agents get better at reasoning, planning, and self-correction every quarter. Human steering is a bottleneck that reintroduces exactly the latency, cost, and attention the automation was built to avoid. The honest position is that steering has a *temporary* value at most — a crutch until capability catches up.

The debate must decide: **will human steering be needed, what value does it provide, and is that value durable or temporary?**

## Why This Matters

If steering is needed, agentic systems must be designed around human interaction points from day one — the "what" (which interactions), "how" (in what form), and "when" (at what moments) all follow from the "why". If it is only a stopgap, designing those interaction points in is premature investment in a legacy mechanism.

## The Case FOR Steering

- **Judgment, not information.** Goals encode taste and values. No amount of model capability produces the *decision that the goal is the right goal* — that is a human value judgment.
- **Wrong-goal failure is the expensive failure.** Relentless execution against a wrong goal wastes money and trust at scale. A single steer early costs little; detection after the fact costs everything.
- **Accountability.** Someone must own the outcome. A process no one steered has no owner; enterprise adoption and audit demand attributable human decisions.
- **Attention is not the constraint — recognition is.** Evidence from computer-use oversight suggests humans failed to intervene not from lack of control but from not recognizing the moment as decision-worthy. Steering is about making those moments visible, not about asking often.

## The Case AGAINST Steering

- **Capability trajectory.** Planning, self-correction, and introspection improve steadily. The judgment gap narrows every release; designing for it now bets on a moving target.
- **The bottleneck returns.** Every interaction point is a place where a process waits on a human. Bounded attention, context-switching, and delay are real costs that compound.
- **Rubber-stamping.** People approve what the machine already decided. A steering mechanism that produces ritual approval adds latency without adding quality — worse than none.
- **Scale.** Human steering does not scale to thousands of parallel agentic processes. If the future is fleets of agents, a human per process is an architectural dead end.

## What the Debate Should Produce

1. Whether human steering is needed — now, and in the near future.
2. What value it concretely provides, and how that value would be observable.
3. Whether that value is durable or temporary.
4. *If* steering is justified: the minimal set of interaction points that would deliver its value (the "what") — but only as a consequence of the argument, never as an assumption.

The panelists hold genuinely opposing positions. Each must argue their side and be challenged by the others.
