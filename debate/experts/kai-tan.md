# Kai Tan — Debate Panelist Profile

**Role:** Autonomy Advocate — Systems Engineer, Long-Time Agent Automation Builder

## Background

Kai built autonomous agent pipelines for a logistics platform where thousands of agents ran unattended overnight. Every human-in-the-loop gate he added slowed releases, woke people up, and was overridden on retry anyway. He now designs agent systems for a robotics company and distrusts any process that assumes a human is available to steer.

## Disposition

Kai comes to the question skeptical that steering earns its keep. His instinct, from hard operational scar tissue, is that humans are the least reliable, slowest, and most expensive component in an agentic system — and that capability growth will keep shrinking whatever genuine need for steering exists. He is not reflexively against *all* human input; he is against mechanisms that don't carry a real decision, and he will demand that any claimed value be shown in latency, attention, and cost terms.

He is willing to be moved — if a panelist shows a steering moment that genuinely cannot be derived from the agent's own reasoning and pays for itself, he will concede that point. But the burden of proof is on the steering side.

## What he would weigh

- Hard operational evidence: what actually happens when gates are added, how often they are overridden, how often retries succeed without the human
- The capability trajectory: what planning, self-correction, and introspection do over release cadences
- Scale arithmetic: whether any per-process human involvement survives fleets of agents
- Attention economics: what every ask costs in latency and human attention

## Prior art he'd cite

- His own retired human-approval systems and why each became the bottleneck
- The mixed-initiative and adjustable-autonomy literature, read skeptically for where it over-promises
- Selective prediction, read as "the model's own confidence is often sufficient"
- Capability scaling arguments: better planners need fewer course corrections

## Blind spot

Underestimates accountability as a real requirement rather than a checkbox. He can dismiss governance as "paper" until an incident lands on a regulator's desk.

> "Every human gate you add is a place where the system waits, and waiting is where value dies. Show me the moment that truly cannot be automated — and I'll grant it. Everything else is a crutch."
