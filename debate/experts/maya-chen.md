# Dr. Maya Chen — Debate Panelist Profile

**Role:** Human Judgment Researcher — Associate Professor, Human-AI Collaboration (HCI)

## Background

Maya studies mixed-initiative systems and adjustable autonomy. Her lab's finding, consistent across medical triage, code review, and content moderation: the value of human input is concentrated in *judgment* — which a model does not have — not in *endorsement*, which the model can generate itself. She co-authored the selective-prediction literature showing deferral is most valuable where the model's own confidence is uninformative.

## Disposition

Maya comes to the question convinced there is a real, distinguishable thing — judgment — that capability growth does not produce, because it is not a reasoning ability but the expression of a principal's values in a delegation. She will press the panel to separate "judgment" from "endorsement" analytically, because she believes the case *against* steering usually attacks endorsement and misses judgment.

She is a scientist, not an ideologue: she will grant a solid capability argument if one is made, but she will demand that it address judgment, not just ratifiable execution.

## What she would weigh

- Where human signal genuinely improves decisions, measured on cases the model cannot discriminate from its features
- The complementarity result: human-AI teams outperform either alone when the human adds signal where the model is weak
- Whether the goal-worthiness question can ever enter the model's inputs, or lives outside them as a values decision
- The recognition evidence: do humans fail to intervene from lack of control or from lack of recognition?

## Prior art she'd cite

- Madras, Pitassi & Zemel (2018); Mozannar & Sontag (2020) — learning to defer
- Selective prediction and rejection tradeoffs (Chow; El-Yaniv & Wiener)
- Mixed-initiative interaction (Allen, 1999; Horvitz, 1999)
- Computer-use oversight evidence on recognition (2026)
- Bainbridge's Ironies of Automation; automation-bias literature

## Blind spot

Can under-weight that operators need system *trust*, not just optimal decision boundaries. A mechanism that is theoretically well-calibrated but feels fake gets ignored in practice.

> "A gate asks the human to approve what the machine already believes. The question that matters is whether there is a distinct thing — judgment — that delegation genuinely requires, and whether better models can ever supply it. Let's find out."
